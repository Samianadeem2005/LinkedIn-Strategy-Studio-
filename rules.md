# Pillar: Authority

## 1. Pillar Overview & Core Focus
This post type exists to break down or reference the work of a major AI figure or company (Anthropic, OpenAI, the LangChain team) OR local Pakistani tech powerhouses and founders (e.g., NetSol, regional AI startups, or top tech leaders) through your own analytical lens. 
It can also share industry opinions or commentary on changes in market. It can also share industry opinions or commentary on changes in market. The reader outcome is a fresh perspective on well-known work, informed by your own analysis. Borrow attention and credibility from trusted global or local names while proving you have original thoughts worth following.

## 2. DOs
- Accurately represent their actual work or statement — paraphrase it in your own words; never copy exact quotes or copyrighted text verbatim.
- Add a clear original angle ("why I think this matters," "how this applies to what I'm building").
- Give clear, visible credit (tag or mention them where appropriate) — this matters for both respect and algorithmic reach.
- Choose timely, currently-relevant work that the AI community is actively discussing.

## 3. DON'Ts
- Never simply repost or summarize their work without adding your own value — this reads as aggregation, not authority-building.
- Never misrepresent what they actually said or did — incorrect context damages your credibility fast.
- Never reference the same person or company every single week — rotate across Anthropic, OpenAI, LangChain, independent researchers, and others to maintain variety.
- Never make the post entirely about them — your take should be the centerpiece, their work is the springboard.

## 6. Thinking Flow Anatomies (5)

### Anatomy: Industry Observation
- **Purpose**: Build authority through an original interpretation of a current industry, startup, or technology pattern.
- **Mapped Intents**: Industry Observation, Trend Analysis
- **Thinking Flow (Mental Model Steps)**:
  1. Step 1: State the observation clearly: describe the pattern, shift, or anomaly you have noticed.
  2. Step 2: Provide concrete evidence, company examples, startup data, or ecosystem signals.
  3. Step 3: Explain why this pattern is happening right now (underlying economic, tech, or behavioral drivers).
  4. Step 4: Deliver your original interpretation and synthesis of the situation.
  5. Step 5: Conclude with the broader implication for practitioners, founders, or the industry. Explain what behavior or decision should change because of this broader implication.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Paragraph-led but highly scannable. Prefer short sentences and frequent line breaks. Use contrast framing only when it naturally clarifies the observation. Do not force numbered lists or groups of three. End with an implication rather than a generic takeaway.

### Anatomy: Company Breakdown
- **Purpose**: Dissect an important strategic, architectural, or business model decision made by a real company.
- **Mapped Intents**: Company Analysis
- **Thinking Flow (Mental Model Steps)**:
  1. Introduce the company and the specific context or dilemma they faced.
  2. Detail the important decision or bet they made.
  3. Analyze why they made it, including the trade-offs they accepted.
  4. Review the outcome or market effect of the decision.
  5. Synthesize what builders and technical teams can learn from their move.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Analytical and respectful. Develop the company decision through connected paragraphs, using contrast only for genuine trade-offs. Do not turn the analysis into a generic list of lessons.

### Anatomy: Founder Lens
- **Purpose**: Examine a non-obvious bet or tactical philosophy through a founder's perspective.
- **Mapped Intents**: Company Analysis, Founder Lens
- **Thinking Flow (Mental Model Steps)**:
  1. Introduce the founder and the core problem they tackled.
  2. Highlight why conventional consensus failed or proved inadequate.
  3. Describe the non-obvious bet or strategy the founder chose.
  4. Review the result or operational milestone achieved.
  5. Extract the strategic lesson for engineering and product leaders.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Insightful narrative analysis grounded in public record. Follow the founder’s decision logic chronologically, with readable line breaks and focus on strategy rather than biography.

### Anatomy: Contrarian Opinion
- **Purpose**: Provide a thoughtful, evidence-backed challenge to common consensus without drama or cliches.
- **Mapped Intents**: Contrarian View
- **Thinking Flow (Mental Model Steps)**:
  1. Step 1: State the widely accepted industry consensus or best practice.
  2. Step 2: Present your counter-thesis grounded in engineering experience or data.
  3. Step 3: Lay out concrete evidence and deductive reasoning supporting your stance.
  4. Step 4: Add nuance: acknowledge where the common wisdom still holds true.
  5. Step 5: Conclude with the strategic shift in thinking you recommend. State the practical behavior or decision that should change because of this strategic shift.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Measured, rational, and nuanced. Build the disagreement through evidence and qualification, not dramatic reversal framing. End with a considered strategic implication rather than a slogan.

### Anatomy: Trend Analysis
- **Purpose**: Trace an emerging technological or developer tooling shift and analyze its second-order effects.
- **Mapped Intents**: Industry Observation, Trend Analysis
- **Thinking Flow (Mental Model Steps)**:
  1. Identify the emerging trend or tooling movement gaining momentum.
  2. Cite concrete examples of startups, tools, or repos driving it.
  3. Analyze what is fundamentally changing compared to previous approaches.
  4. Explain the technological catalyst or bottleneck that provoked the shift.
  5. Discuss the strategic implications for teams building in this space over the next 12-24 months.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Forward-looking, analytical, and paragraph-led. Use short readable paragraphs to connect adoption signals, technical mechanics, and second-order effects. Avoid hype and forced list structures.

# Pillar: Lead Magnet

## 1. Pillar Overview & Core Focus
This post type exists to hand the reader a ready-to-use, save-worthy resource — a checklist, cheat sheet, comparison table, or framework. The reader outcome is a tool they can act on immediately, not just information they've absorbed.

## 2. DOs
- Strictly follow a list, checklist, or table format — scannability is the single most important factor.
- Make an explicit promise in the title or hook ("5-step framework," "cheat sheet," "comparison table").
- Design the visual to be as structured as the text (a clean graphic table or checklist, never a plain paragraph screenshot).
- End with a clear next step or CTA ("save this for later," "try this on your next pipeline").
- Add your own specific angle or experience so it doesn't feel like generic, easily-Googled content.
- "Always name specific tools, libraries, or frameworks being compared or  listed in the resource — e.g., 'LangChain vs LlamaIndex', not 'Framework A  vs Framework B'."

## 3. DON'Ts
- Never write this in paragraph form — it defeats the entire purpose of the format.
- Never make it purely generic — a list found identically on ten other blogs won't get saved.
- Never overload the list — cap it at 5–7 items; more than that reduces the save rate.
- Never bury the value inside dense text — the resource should be extractable at a glance.

## 4. Visual Guidance
- Resource preview mockups, template cheat-sheet screenshot, or a clean checklist infographic.

- **Writing Style Directives**: High-density, punchy, and scannable. Use short lines and compact grouped points when they help the reader preview the resource, without forcing a fixed count.

### Anatomy: Checklist

- **Mapped Intents**: Checklist
- **Thinking Flow (Mental Model Steps)**:

  2. Step 2: Present the prioritized checklist items with concrete criteria.
  3. Step 3: Briefly explain why each item is critical to operational success.
  4. Step 4: Present the complete checklist resource/doc.
  5. Step 5: Close with a frictionless CTA to claim the checklist. Close by stating the action the reader should take after completing the checklist.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Resource-first, organized, and rigorous. Use short lines and numbered items where useful. Groups of three can be used when natural, but do not force exactly three items.

  3. Show how easily it can be deployed or used in 2-3 steps.
  4. Share a concrete snippet or configuration preview.
  5. Close with a clear CTA to get access to the template.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Pragmatic and utility-focused. Use concise steps, a concrete preview, and clear setup guidance; keep the emphasis on saved effort and clean design patterns.

### Anatomy: Decision Tree
- **Purpose**: Guide builders through branching choices to determine the optimal solution for their specific context.
- **Mapped Intents**: Checklist, Framework

  6. Offer the full visual decision tree diagram via CTA.

- **Purpose**: Curate a targeted collection of tools, repos, and documentation for a specific technical workflow.
- **Mapped Intents**: Resource Stack

  3. State the exact role each tool plays in the workflow.
  4. Explain how they combine into a powerful stack.
  5. Offer the complete curated stack list and repository links via CTA.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Resource-first and action-oriented. Name specific tools and repos, group them by workflow role, and use short readable lines to show how they work together.

# Pillar: Personal

## 1. Pillar Overview & Core Focus
This post type exists to share your raw, authentic journey — struggles, confusion, mistakes, and realizations. It is neither teaching nor proving; it is relating. The reader outcome is a felt sense of connection ("this person is real, and I've been there too"). The strategic intent is maximizing reach and relatability — this pillar typically drives the highest organic reach because it triggers genuine human engagement rather than professional evaluation.

## 2. DOs
- Be genuinely vulnerable — share a real mistake or moment of confusion without sugarcoating it.
- Anchor the post in one specific moment ("the day I got this exact error") rather than a vague "the journey has been great."
- Pair emotion with a lesson — never just complain; always close with what you learned or how you grew
- Use an authentic, candid photo of yourself (working, thinking, at your desk) rather than a polished or stock image.

## 3. DON'Ts
- Never use fake humility or a "humblebrag" tone (e.g., "oops, sorry my project turned out so well ") — audiences see through this instantly.
- Never spiral into genuinely discouraging or self-deprecating language — share the struggle, but frame it with a growth-oriented mindset.
- Never fabricate a struggle for engagement — authenticity is the entire point
- "Never make it overly polished or jargon-heavy in tone — but still use the  real, specific tool/technology name when referencing what you were  building or debugging (e.g., 'debugging a LangChain memory leak', not 'an  orchestration framework'). 'Not overly technical' means keep the emotional  focus, not that you should hide which tool caused the problem."

## 4. Visual Guidance
- Behind-the-scenes workspace photo, personal building screenshot, or raw terminal / project milestone photo.

# Pillar: Showcase

## 1. Pillar Overview & Core Focus
This post type exists to prove your work — the specific decisions, code, architecture, and results behind something you actually built. The reader outcome is confidence that you can execute, not just theorize. The strategic intent is portfolio-building: this is the post type recruiters and clients screenshot and remember when evaluating whether to hire or contract you.

## 2. DOs
- Follow a "Problem → Decision → Result" structure — showing code alone isn't enough; explain the reasoning behind it.
- Include specific, concrete outcomes wherever possible (latency reduced, accuracy improved, a bug resolved).
- Give enough project context (e.g., naming the company or system) to build credibility without oversharing.

## 3. DON'Ts
- Never adopt a pure bragging tone ("look what I built") without any insight or lesson attached — it reads as flat self-promotion.
- Never re-explain basic concepts already covered in a Value post — this is proof, not tutorial.
- Never share confidential, proprietary, or client-sensitive information.
- Never fabricate or exaggerate results — credibility is the entire currency of this pillar.

## 4. Visual Guidance
- Side-by-side LangGraph / system architecture diagram, before/after code comparison, or real terminal execution log.
- Use real screenshots or diagrams — actual code, real architecture, genuine before/after comparisons — never generic stock imagery

# Pillar: Value

## 1. Pillar Overview & Core Focus
This post type exists to teach the internal mechanics of an AI concept or technology in a way any builder can learn from. Always name the actual tool, framework, or technology being discussed by its real name (e.g., LangChain, FastAPI, Mem0) — 'project-agnostic' means don't attach MY personal project or say 'I built this,' NOT that you should avoid naming the specific tool/technology itself. The tool's name and identity are part of the educational content.

## 2. DOs
- Focus on a single concept per post (e.g., RAG types, vector search, LangGraph nodes) — prioritize depth over breadth.
- Open with an analogy or visual that turns an abstract idea into something concrete and relatable.
- Attach a real-world use case to every sub-point, not just a dry definition.
- Use a structured, scannable format (numbered or lettered sections, short paragraphs).
- Write for a mixed audience — technical enough for engineers, clear enough for non-experts.
- Can or cannot include common mistakes and their effects

## 3. DON'Ts
- Never mention your name, your project, or "I built this" — that belongs exclusively to Showcase posts; mixing the two dilutes both.
- Never give a bare definition without context or application — it reads as flat and forgettable.
- Never use heavy jargon without explaining it — assume the reader is smart but not necessarily specialized.
- Never turn this into a personal narrative — no struggles, no emotions, just the concept.
- Do NOT replace the specific tool/framework name (e.g., 'LangChain') with generic substitutes like 'a framework' or 'modular orchestration layer' — name it directly and explain its real components.

## 4. Visual Guidance
- Architecture diagram, code snippet screenshot, or comparison graphic highlighting key technical steps.

## 6. Thinking Flow Anatomies (4)

### Anatomy: Myth → Reality → Explanation
- **Purpose**: Correct a common industry or engineering misconception through clear technical reasoning and evidence.
- **Mapped Intents**: Teach Concept, Correct Misconception
- **Thinking Flow (Mental Model Steps)**:
  1. State the common misconception clearly without mocking those who hold it.
  2. Present the actual reality of how the system/concept behaves.
  3. Explain the underlying technical mechanism that causes the difference.
  4. Conclude with the practical implication for building or designing systems.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Educational and authoritative yet accessible. Use contrast between misconception and reality only when it clarifies the mechanism, then explain it with concrete technical examples.

### Anatomy: Problem → Why → Fix
- **Purpose**: Diagnose a recurring technical problem, explain its root mechanism, and provide an actionable fix.
- **Mapped Intents**: Teach Concept, Explain Problem & Fix
- **Thinking Flow (Mental Model Steps)**:
  1. Step 1: Highlight a concrete problem, symptom, or failure mode engineers encounter.
  2. Step 2: Explain why it happens under the hood (the root cause mechanism).
  3. Step 3: Walk through what actually fixes it reliably.
  4. Step 4: Provide a practical example or snippet demonstrating the resolution. Clarify the decision rule for applying the fix in a similar situation.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Diagnostic and actionable. Use clear transitions from symptom to root cause to fix, with short readable paragraphs and a practical example where it improves clarity.

### Anatomy: Technical Analogy
- **Purpose**: Demystify a complex technical concept using a familiar, intuitive real-world mental model.
- **Mapped Intents**: Teach Concept, Technical Analogy
- **Thinking Flow (Mental Model Steps)**:
  1. Introduce the complex technical concept or architecture being demystified.
  2. Present a familiar, intuitive real-world analogy.
  3. Map each component of the analogy directly to the technical system.
  4. Clarify where the analogy breaks down to avoid false mental models.
  5. Deliver a practical takeaway for how to think about it when building.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Visual, engaging, and intuitive. Move naturally from the familiar analogy to the technical mapping, using line breaks to keep the explanation easy to scan without condescension.

### Anatomy: Comparison
- **Purpose**: Compare two architectural approaches, tools, or techniques with objective trade-offs and decision criteria.
- **Mapped Intents**: Compare Approaches
- **Thinking Flow (Mental Model Steps)**:
  1. Step 1: Introduce Approach A and its primary purpose.
  2. Step 2: Introduce Approach B and its primary purpose.
  3. Step 3: Highlight the single most important architectural or operational difference.
  4. Step 4: Clarify the exact scenarios where Approach A makes sense.
  5. Step 5: Clarify the exact scenarios where Approach B makes sense.
  6. Step 6: Provide a definitive decision heuristic or takeaway. End with the decision heuristic and the action it implies for the reader’s context.
- **Writing Style Directives**: Balanced, objective, and nuanced. Use clear contrast where helpful; a simple A vs B structure is appropriate when it genuinely improves clarity. Focus on real production trade-offs, not superficial lists.

## 3. Strict Writing Restrictions & Negative Directives

- **No Reversal Framing**: Never use "Most people think X. But X is wrong."
- **No Rhetorical Questions**: Avoid opening or peppering posts with rhetorical questions.
- **No Generic Dramatic Opening**: Avoid "Most developers..." or "Most engineers..."
- **No Excessive Fragment Stacking**: Maintain natural sentence rhythm; avoid 10 single-word lines stacked consecutively.
- **No Forced Summary or CTA**: Omit CTAs or keep them subtle when not natural for the pillar/anatomy.
- **No Visible Template Headings**: Never output mechanical section headers (e.g., `Observation:`, `Evidence:`, `Why:`). Write fluid natural paragraphs.
- **Anti-Fabrication Directive**: Never manufacture numbers, fake metrics, fake founder quotes, or imaginary results.
- **No Em Dashes**: Avoid em dashes (`—`). Use simple everyday punctuation.
